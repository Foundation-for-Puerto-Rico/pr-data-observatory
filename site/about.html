About this initiative

What is the PR Data Observatory?

A living, open model of Puerto Rico. It puts public data from many fields on one map and one timeline so you can see how things connect: where people live and what is near them, what things cost and what people earn, what is exposed to risk, and how all of it has changed.

Developed by Foundation for Puerto Rico as a tool for public use and benefit. It is free, needs no account, and is built from open and public data.

What it covers

    The observatory keeps growing: a new field appears here and in the Overview when data for it is added. Fields marked “no data yet” are planned.

    Who it is for

    • Residents and community groups: see how your municipality compares with others and what is near where you live.
    • Local and island planners: find where people live far from services, test a new transit route and compare municipalities side by side.
    • Researchers, students and journalists: every number has a definition, a source and a way to cite it in the data dictionary, and tables download as CSV.
    • Funders, nonprofits and recovery teams: set money, needs and risks side by side to see where they meet.

    Where the data come from

    Public data from the US Census Bureau, Bureau of Labor Statistics, FEMA, NOAA, USGS, FAA, the Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA), the Junta de Planificación, the Autoridad de Transporte Integrado and municipal transit feeds, OpenStreetMap, Overture Maps, and FPR's own 2023 housing study. Most have an open license, but not all: some sources, such as the municipal transit feeds and the Junta de Planificación's maps, state no license. The Data management page gives the license class and review of every file.

    The data dictionary lists every source with its license, the credit line to use and how often it changes, and how to cite the observatory and any variable.

    Keep in mind

    • It is a snapshot. The label at the top says which one. Transit timetables refresh every night; the rest is rebuilt in snapshots. Each dataset shows the year of its data.
    • Places are listings, not a census of businesses. Tourism places come from Overture Maps and can be missing, duplicated or out of date. The confidence slider filters them.
    • Hazards are mapped zones. A flood zone is FEMA's map of risk at a point, not a prediction, and it says nothing about a building's elevation or insurance.
    • Timetables are not live. Real-time bus positions are not public today. “On their timetable” is a schedule calculation.
    • Survey numbers are estimates. Margins of error are large in small municipalities. Jobs are counted where the work is done, not where people live, and informal work is not counted.
    • Patterns are not causes. Use the observatory to ask better questions and plan, not as a legal, financial or engineering determination.

    Privacy

    There are no accounts, cookies or analytics. What you type in the search box or the chat stays in your browser; the one exception is “Look up as an address online”, which sends that text to OpenStreetMap's Nominatim service (nominatim.openstreetmap.org).

    To draw the pages, your browser also asks other servers for files. Each server receives your IP address, the browser's usual HTTP headers and the file or tile it sends; a map tile shows which area of the map you are viewing. These are the servers:

    • Microsoft Azure (Azure Static Web Apps), which hosts the observatory itself: every page and its data.
    • Google Fonts (fonts.googleapis.com, fonts.gstatic.com): the fonts, on every page.
    • cdnjs (cdnjs.cloudflare.com): code libraries (Leaflet, MapLibre, Chart.js) on the map, 3D and Housing pages and the barrio vulnerability page.
    • unpkg (unpkg.com): the deck.gl and PMTiles libraries of the 3D view.
    • OpenFreeMap (tiles.openfreemap.org, served through Cloudflare): the base map's style, vector tiles, icons and label fonts, on the map, 3D and barrio vulnerability pages.
    • Amazon Web Services (s3.amazonaws.com): the terrain elevation tiles of the 3D view.
    • NOAA (coast.noaa.gov): the sea level rise tiles, on the map and 3D pages, only while that layer is on.

    How to use the Observatory

    Eight short steps. Go through them one at a time, or read them all. You can replay the tutorial whenever you like.

    1. Start on the map

      Map opens on its Home tab: ten topics, each with a headline figure for the whole island. Open a topic to see its maps, or search all the data.

    2. Put data on the map

      Add a dataset from a topic, from Map layers or from the Data page. The On the map panel lists every layer that is on, with its legend and period. Change, hide or remove a layer there.

    3. Move through time

      The timeline under the map sets the year for every dataset on it. Press play to watch the years, or Latest to return. Each layer says the period it really shows.

    4. Read the Insights

      The Insights panel beside the map gives a key figure, a trend, the spread of values, the regions and the highest and lowest municipios for a dataset on the map. Click a region or drag across the histogram to filter those figures.

    5. Look at a place

      Click a municipality or a place on the map. The Place tab shows its profile, the profile of a region and the rankings of all 78 municipios.

    6. Switch between 2D and 3D

      The 2D / 3D switch above the map changes the view of the same analysis. 3D adds terrain, buildings, a day of transit, storms and sea level by year. A layer that one view cannot draw is named, and it comes back when you return.

    7. Analyze with the tools

      The Tools tab holds the map tools, the transit tools and the workbench under the map: a table, charts, comparisons, indexes and scenarios. Workflows runs step-by-step counts on the published files.

    8. Save, share and check

      Save a map in this browser or copy its link from the bar above the map; Map Gallery lists your saved maps and ready-made ones. The Data dictionary says what every number means, where it comes from and how to cite it.
