Puerto Rico's reservoirs: how full they are now

The water level of 25 reservoirs, read live from the US Geological Survey, compared with the same week in 2011 to 2025. A reservoir is lower than usual when its level is below what it had in nine of every ten of those years for this week. The 19 reservoirs in the daily report of the Aqueduct and Sewer Authority (AAA) give Puerto Rico's overall picture.

Read with care. The water in each tank is where the reservoir's level sits between its lowest and highest level of 2011-2025: a level, not a volume. USGS publishes no volumes, and sediment has filled part of several reservoirs, so the same level holds less water than it once did. 'Usual' compares a reservoir with its own past, not with a safe or unsafe line: the Aqueduct and Sewer Authority (AAA) sets the official alert levels, linked from each reservoir.

infoMethod

The level. The water-surface elevation of each reservoir, in metres above local mean sea level (USGS parameter 72376), read live from USGS when the page opens, with the daily values since the file was built. On 2 November 2026 these matched AAA's morning chart to within 0.08 m for 18 of its 19 reservoirs (the check is in docs/facilities.md of the project repository).
Usual for this time of year. For each week of the year, the mean level of that week in each year from 2011 to 2025. Lower than usual is below the 10th percentile of those yearly values, higher than usual above the 90th, usual in between, at least 8 years. The ranking is the share of those years with a lower level than today. The map's Reservoir levels layer uses the same rule.
The water in the tanks. Where today's level sits between the reservoir's lowest and highest daily level of 2011 to 2025: 0% at its lowest, 100% at its highest. The shaded band in a large tank is the usual range for this week. Puerto Rico's tank is the median of the 19 reservoirs in AAA's report. It is not a share of the reservoir's volume.
The history. The monthly mean level since each gauge's first daily value (the oldest in 1987); a month needs 15 days of readings. Older years on another reference, or while a reservoir was still filling, are left out: Toa Vaca reported a depth, not an elevation, until 2005, and Cerrillos was filling in 1991-1992.
What the page does not do. It does not say whether there will be water rationing, how many days of water are left, or how much water a reservoir holds. AAA decides rationing with its own alert levels, and its daily report is on its website. A reservoir can be lowered on purpose for repairs, dredging or before a storm.
