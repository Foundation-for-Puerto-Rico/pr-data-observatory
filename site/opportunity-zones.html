Opportunity Zones 2.0 in Puerto Rico

What Opportunity Zones are and why they matter

An Opportunity Zone is a census tract with low incomes or high poverty where federal tax law rewards long-term private investment. A person or company that sells an asset at a profit (a capital gain) can put that money into a Qualified Opportunity Fund, which invests in businesses, housing or buildings in the zones. In exchange, the federal tax on that gain is deferred for up to five years and can be reduced, and the growth of the investment itself is not taxed if it is held ten years or more. Congress created the zones in 2017 and made them permanent in 2025, with a new round every ten years.

The incentive exists to bring private capital to places that have trouble attracting it: the IRS describes the zones as designed to spur economic development and job creation in distressed communities. Because the benefit goes to investors who stay for years, it favours long-term projects, such as new or larger businesses, housing, commercial buildings and infrastructure, that can create jobs and services where they are scarce. For municipios, community organizations and entrepreneurs, a project inside a zone can offer investors a tax advantage that a project elsewhere cannot.

A designation alone does not bring investment. Treasury's Office of Tax Analysis counts $112 billion invested through Opportunity Funds through 2024, reaching 77% of the zones in the states; more than half of it was in real estate, and more of it went to less distressed areas and to areas that were already growing. In Puerto Rico, where over 90% of census tracts were zones in the first round, only 7% of those zones had received investment by 2024. From 2027 there are fewer zones, 178, and rural zones offer larger benefits, so knowing exactly where they are helps plan projects and bring investors to them.

The 2026 designation

On November 5, 2026, the US Treasury designated 178 census tracts of Puerto Rico as Opportunity Zones for ten years from January 1, 2027: 96 not rural and 82 rural. They were home to 676,727 people, 20.6% of Puerto Rico's residents in the 2020 Census, and each of the 78 municipios has at least one. In the first round, 863 tracts were zones, with 97.5% of the residents of the 2010 Census; those zones end on December 31, 2027.

What a zone is not. It is not a grant: no public money is paid to the zone or to its residents. This page describes the federal designation; it does not judge which tracts were chosen.

location_city3 · By barrio

A barrio can lie in several census tracts, some in a zone and some not, so part of its residents can live in a zone. The residents are counted by census block, so the barrios add up exactly to the municipios. Pick a municipio to see all its barrios.

scatter_plot4 · How a tract qualified

A tract was eligible if it was a low-income community under the law as amended in 2025: a median family income of at most 70% of its area's, or a poverty rate of 20% or more with a median family income of at most 125% of its area's. The area is the metropolitan or micropolitan area the tract is in, or Puerto Rico outside them. Treasury used the American Community Survey of 2020-2024. 712 of Puerto Rico's tracts were eligible, with 76.0% of its residents. Puerto Rico's government could nominate a quarter of them, rounded up: 178. It nominated 178 and Treasury designated all of them.

The medians of each group, from the figures Treasury published. They describe the tracts; they do not explain why a tract was or was not nominated.

history5 · The first round and the second

In 2018, a rule for Puerto Rico alone made every low-income tract a zone, so nearly the whole island was one. The 2025 law ended that rule: from 2027, Puerto Rico follows the same limit as the states. The first round's zones stay in effect until December 31, 2027, so during 2027 both sets apply. The two rounds use different census tracts (2010 and 2020), so they are compared by counts and shares, not tract by tract.

savings6 · What the incentive offers investors

For investments made after December 31, 2026. A taxpayer who invests a capital gain in a Qualified Opportunity Fund pays the federal tax on that gain when the investment is sold, or five years after it was made, whichever comes first. If the investment is held five years, the gain taxed then is 10% smaller (30% smaller for a fund that invests in rural zones). If it is held ten years or more, the growth of the investment itself is not taxed, up to 30 years. A fund must hold at least 90% of its assets in property, stock or partnership interests in zones.
Rural zones. A zone is rural when it lies entirely outside any city or town of more than 50,000 inhabitants and the urban areas joined to it; in Puerto Rico, Treasury used the census designated places of more than 50,000 inhabitants as the cities. Besides the larger tax cut, a building bought in a rural zone qualifies when it is improved by half of what it cost, not counting the land, instead of by all of it (IRS Notice 2025-50).
What the page does not show. Where Opportunity Fund money has gone: Treasury publishes the investment in zones only in aggregate studies, not by tract. Nor Puerto Rico's own incentives, or how federal tax applies to a resident of Puerto Rico; ask a tax adviser for that.

infoMethod

The lists. The designated tracts, their rural status and the figures of every tract are Treasury's (Data Transparency files of October 30, 2026), checked against the CDFI Fund's full list of November 5, 2026: Puerto Rico's 178 tracts and their rural status are the same in both. Some announcements gave 97 tracts that are not rural; both lists have 96, for 178 in all.
Eligibility. The law's test, applied to Treasury's published poverty rate and median family income, gives exactly Treasury's list of eligible tracts; tracts with no income figure were judged by the poverty test alone. The figures are survey estimates with margins of error, and Treasury's file is the official list.
Residents. Residents and housing units are those of the 2020 Census. Each census block is counted whole in its tract and, by its internal point, in its barrio, so tracts, barrios and municipios add up exactly. The first round's residents are those of the 2010 Census in its 2010 tracts.
More detail. The method, the checks and the code are in docs/opportunity-zones.md of the project repository; each figure has an entry in the data dictionary (search for 'Opportunity Zones'). The zones are also a layer of the map and of the 3D view, in the group Economy.
