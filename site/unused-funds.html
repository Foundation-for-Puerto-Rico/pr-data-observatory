Federal grants: balances, reductions and audit findings

Of the 7,197 federal grants that began in federal fiscal years 2020 to 2026 for work in Puerto Rico, 3,257 ended more than a year ago. In 564 of them the federal record shows a balance not yet paid out: $1.21 billion of the $3.77 billion obligated to them (2%), in 219 federal programs of 16 federal agencies. A balance on record is not money lost, and by itself it does not show that money is at risk: a grant can be extended, closing a grant often runs late on the federal side, and the record of payments can be behind.

Read with care. An unpaid balance is not proof that money was lost or that anyone did something wrong. The federal record of payments can be late or incomplete, a grant's end date can be extended after the data were read, and some programs pay through other accounts. undo5 · Reductions of the amount committed

After a grant is signed, the federal government can lower the amount committed to it (a de-obligation). The data do not say why an amount was lowered. A reduction can be an administrative adjustment, an adjustment to the actual cost, a federal cancellation, or money that was not committed in time, and the page cannot tell them apart. With that limit: from federal fiscal year 2019 to 2026, 2,533 grants for work in Puerto Rico ended the period below the highest amount committed to them, a net reduction of $2.03 billion in 422 programs of 27 federal agencies.

Not a measure of how grants were carried out. A reduction is not proof that anyone did something wrong, and many come from federal decisions. The page counts all of them the same way.
Net, not gross. USAspending lists 9,858 negative transactions worth $23.39 billion in these years, but most are lowered one day and raised again later. The page counts only what each grant lost for good: its highest level minus its final level.

fact_check6 · Audit findings that repeat

Every body that spends $750,000 or more of federal money in a year must have a single audit. The auditor reports each problem found in a federal program as a finding, and says whether the same finding was in the audit before. From audit year 2019 to 2024, bodies in Puerto Rico filed 1,908 single audits; 720 had at least one finding. Of the 1,659 findings, 843 (51%) were repeated. The share stayed between 46% and 56% every year.

A finding is not a loss. A finding says a rule was not followed or a control was weak. It does not say money was misused or taken back, and many findings are small. The page counts findings; it does not weigh them. Questioned costs appear only as a yes or no: the source publishes no amount.
Why repeated findings matter. A repeated finding is the closest open measure of whether a problem found one year was corrected by the next. It does not say why it was not: some corrections take more than a year.

paymentsFive grants rebuilt, one by one

Before building any new measure, the page follows five large grants through everything the open record gives: each change of the amount committed and each year's payments. It is a test of the source: how far can a balance on record be trusted? The five were not chosen by hand. They are the grants with the largest balance on record among those that ended more than a year ago, one for each of the first five programs, read on November 20, 2026.

What the five show. In 3 of the 5, more than half of everything paid was paid after the fiscal year of the end date. In one, payments stop in the record two years before the grant ended. In one, the total paid went down. None of the five has a closing date, an approved extension or a decision of the federal agency in the record: USAspending does not publish them.
What follows from it. A balance after the end date tells where to ask, not how much money went without use. The end date on record is often not the last day to pay, and the record of payments can begin late, stop early or be corrected. For that reason the page builds no indicator from these balances, and the figures of sections 1 to 4 are a count of what the record shows, with these limits.

helpWhat this page cannot answer

What open data cannot answer. Why a balance was not used, what services were not delivered, and how much Commonwealth money replaced a federal payment that was denied. No public data source has these.

infoMethod

Stage and basis. Obligated and paid out by the federal government, by grant; reported in USAspending, not audited. Grants signed in federal fiscal years 2020 to 2026 (October to September), with place of performance Puerto Rico.
The grants read. Grants that began in federal fiscal years 2020 to 2026. USAspending's search also returns older grants that were only amended in those years; those 1,250 grants are left out, because USAspending has no payments for them before fiscal year 2020.
Ended. A grant's end date closes its period of performance. The data were read on November 19, 2026, and a grant counts as ended when its end date is before November 19, 2025, more than one year earlier. The one-year line follows the closeout rule (2 CFR 200.344): the recipient has 120 days to pay its bills and send its reports (90 days for grants made before November 12, 2020), and the agency should make every effort to close the grant within a year. Passing that line does not show that the balance was lost: the time to pay out can be extended, and closing is often late on the federal side.
Unpaid balance. The amount obligated minus what the federal Treasury has paid out, never below zero. A grant is counted when its balance is at least 5% of the grant and at least $1,000. 79 ended grants ($71.2 million) report no payment at all; they are shown apart in section 4, because a blank is not a zero.
Net reduction. For money taken back, the page reads every transaction on grants with place of performance Puerto Rico from federal fiscal year 2019 to 2026: 74,665 transactions on 13,838 grants. A negative transaction is a de-obligation. For each grant, the transactions of one day count as one change; the net reduction is the highest level the grant reached in the period minus the level where it ended. Grants with less than $1,000 are left out.
Money moved, not taken back. When a reduction on one grant is matched by an equal increase on another grant of the same program and agency within 31 days, the money was moved. Those 184 reductions ($337.5 million) are not counted.
Audit findings. The page reads every single audit filed by a body in Puerto Rico from audit year 2019 in the Federal Audit Clearinghouse: 2,188 audits. The source lists a finding once for each award line it touches; here a finding is counted once per audit, and it is repeated, a material weakness or has questioned costs when any of its lines says so. Bodies are counted by the kind the source records and are never named.
What the page leaves out. Contracts, loans and direct payments to people. Grants signed before fiscal year 2020, because USAspending has no payments for them. The name of any recipient: this step counts by federal agency and program only.
More detail. The method, the tests and the code are in docs/unused-funds.md of the project repository. The files data/unused_funds.json, data/taken_back.json and data/audit_findings.json have every program line.
