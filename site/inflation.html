Inflation in Puerto Rico and the United States

In September 2026, consumer prices in Puerto Rico were 3.8% higher than a year before; in the United States, 3.7%. From January 2000 to September 2026, prices rose 74.9% in Puerto Rico and 99.6% in the United States. What markets and forecasters expect for US prices implies about 2.8% a year for both from 2027 to 2031, with a wide range.

BLS did not publish October 2025, so the US rate for October 2026 is not shown. The bands are how far this method's forecasts have missed in the past (see The projection).

In short. For most of the last 25 years Puerto Rico's measured inflation ran below the US rate: on average 1.89% a year against 2.63% since the end of 2006. The two move together, because Puerto Rico uses the dollar, shares the Federal Reserve's policy and imports most of what it consumes. In 6 of the last 12 months, Puerto Rico's rate was the higher one, led by transportation (9.7% over 12 months).

Income and prices

The median household income in Puerto Rico was $29,272 in 2025 ($18,862 in 2010); in the United States, $84,780. After removing each place's inflation, Puerto Rico's median rose 24.0% from 2010 to 2025 and the US median 14.7%. Most of Puerto Rico's gain came after 2019. A median describes the middle household; many households saw less, and the figures are survey estimates with margins of error.

savingsBuying power by household income

When prices rise and income does not, the same income buys less. Enter a household's yearly income; it starts at Puerto Rico's median household income in 2025, $29,272, the latest from the Census Bureau. Choose a starting month to see what that income buys today at that month's prices, the raise it would need to buy the same, and how much more it would lose by December 2031 if it stays the same.

The median is the income of the household in the middle; it is used instead of the average because a few high incomes pull the average up. Every income loses the same share here, because there is one price index for all families. Lower-income households spend more of their income on food, housing, electricity and transportation, so when those prices rise faster (transportation: 9.7% over 12 months) they lose more; DTRH does not publish an index by income.

elderlySocial Security in Puerto Rico

Social Security benefits rise each December by a cost-of-living adjustment set from the US consumer price index for wage earners (CPI-W), the same everywhere. Because Puerto Rico's prices rose less than US prices, the adjustments bought more in Puerto Rico: a benefit of $1,000 in January 2007 was $1,594.96 by September 2026 after the adjustments; at Puerto Rico's prices of January 2007 that is $1,104.46, and at US prices $958.17.

The adjustments are computed here from the CPI-W with Social Security's rule (the July to September average over the same months of the last year with an adjustment, rounded to a tenth of a point); they match the adjustments Social Security announced from 2007 to 2025.

query_stats4 · The projection and how reliable it is

The central path for both places is the US consumer inflation that markets and forecasters expect, year by year: the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland's model of October 2026, which reads Treasury yields, inflation swaps and surveys. It expects 2.98% over the next year and 2.77% a year over five. The Survey of Professional Forecasters (2026 Q3) expects 2.43% over the next year and 2.30% a year over ten.

Why use the US expectation for Puerto Rico? Five methods were tested the same way: a forecast from each quarter of 2011 to 2021, using only what was known then, compared with Puerto Rico's actual inflation over the next one to five years. Following the US expectation had the smallest error at every horizon: 1.18 points over five years, against 1.49 for a model fitted to Puerto Rico's own monthly data. That model finds that Puerto Rico's prices pass on US inflation about one for one (a long-run pass-through of 1.11), but its Puerto Rico-specific part did not help forecasts.

Read the bands with care. The 80% band is the central path plus or minus 1.28 times the root mean square error of the same method in the backtest, at each horizon; the 95% band, 1.96 times. For Puerto Rico's rate over the next year that error is about 2.0 points, so its 80% range is about ±2.6 points. Most of that comes from 2021 and 2022, when inflation rose far above almost every forecast. The bands come from a short history and assume the future misses like the past; they are not a probability statement about any one year.
What would move the path. Fuel: transportation is 24% of Puerto Rico's basket, against about a sixth of the US index, and most of Puerto Rico's electricity is made from imported fuel, whose cost reaches bills through the fuel charge, so oil prices move Puerto Rico's rate further. Also electricity rates set by the Energy Bureau, shipping costs and federal tariffs on imports. A change in what markets expect from the Federal Reserve moves the central path of both places.

infoMethod

The indexes. Puerto Rico: the official consumer price index of the Department of Labor and Human Resources (DTRH), all families, base December 2006 = 100, not seasonally adjusted. From 2007 it is the 2010 revision, which averages prices within each item with a geometric mean; before 2007 it is DTRH's earlier index joined to the same base, whose rise from 2005 to 2008 partly reflects its old weights. United States: the BLS consumer price index for all urban consumers (CPI-U), US city average, not seasonally adjusted. The DTRH workbook and DTRH's file on indicadores.pr agree in 230 of 231 shared months.
Not the same measurement. Part of the long-run gap between the two rates is how they measure, not only prices: the geometric mean gives a lower rate than BLS's mostly arithmetic one, DTRH's basket is from 2000-01 while BLS updates its weights every year, and rent and owners' rent-equivalent weigh much less in Puerto Rico's index.
The projection. The Cleveland Fed's expected inflation over one to ten years is turned into the expected inflation of each single year ahead; each place's index is carried forward at those rates from its latest month, with its own seasonal pattern (the average change of each calendar month over the last ten years), so the 12-month rates show only real base effects. The model in the backtest is an ARDL of Puerto Rico's monthly change on its last month, US inflation this month and last month, and month dummies, from 2008 (225 months). For each of the 44 origins, every method saw only data up to that month; the Cleveland Fed re-estimates its model with all data, so its past expectations are close to, but not exactly, what was known then.
What the page does not do. It is not a forecast of FPR or of any agency. It does not project each group, municipio or kind of household, and it does not measure the cost of living in one place against another: an index measures change over time, not the level of prices.
