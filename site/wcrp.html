Whole Community Resilience Planning (WCRP)

WCRP helps communities in Puerto Rico design their own resilience plans: they set their vision, needs and goals, and plan, run and follow up the work to reach them. Foundation for Puerto Rico runs the program in collaboration with the Puerto Rico Department of Housing (PRDOH), with Community Development Block Grant – Disaster Recovery (CDBG-DR) funds from the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD), as part of the Action Plan that HUD approved.

The program description is Foundation for Puerto Rico's.

About this map and its sources

The WCRP Social Capital Maps placed community assets on a map in six themes: health, education, security, Comunidades Especiales, community services and environment. This map shows the same themes with the observatory's current official layers, each with its own date. No contact details or private addresses are shown, and sensitive places are counted, not drawn.

Points: public libraries (USAC E-Rate) and historic places (National Park Service, National Register of Historic Places), fire and EMS stations (USGS), police stations and city halls (OpenStreetMap), public housing developments (HUD). Areas: Comunidades Especiales (Junta de Planificación, 2006) and protected areas (USGS PAD-US). Counted by municipio, never drawn: health facilities, substance use and mental health treatment facilities (SAMHSA), community water systems outside PRASA (EPA), and historic places that are private homes.

The WCRP communities are the 76 points of the observatory's WCRP layer (FPR program list, 2026-10-06); each point is approximate, because the plans map their area only in a PDF.

About this list

The WCRP Social Capital Mapping list records, for each Comunidad Especial, whether it has a community center, a library, an electronic library, a passive park, a sports court, a residents' association, a recreation association, a crime-prevention association and a PDI. The list does not state its date; it was compiled for the program about 2019–2021 from the Junta de Planificación inventory, and no newer source exists. "PDI" is the list's own column name and is not defined there. Click a Comunidad Especial on the map for its own flags.

The Risk and Vulnerability Indicators give each barrio a level from 1 (low) to 5 (high) by comparing it with the other barrios of Puerto Rico: choose an area and click a barrio to see what is behind its level.

Original 2022

Each step after the original uses one five-year Census period. Neighboring periods share four years, so small moves between steps can be survey error.

What the levels mean

  • 1Lowamong the 20% least vulnerable barrios
  • 2Medium-low
  • 3Medium
  • 4Medium-high
  • 5Highamong the 20% most vulnerable barrios
  • No residents or no data

The index compares each barrio with the other barrios in Puerto Rico, using Census and other official data. Level 5 means the barrio is in the fifth with the most disadvantages in the chosen area; level 1, in the fifth with the fewest. It does not measure any one person's need or decide who gets help. Use it to see patterns across groups of barrios.

Read with care
  • A level compares barrios with each other on the inputs used. It is not a measure of need, risk of harm or eligibility for any program.
  • Survey estimates for barrios are rough. Many barrios have few households in the sample, so a barrio near the edge of a fifth can move a level from one survey period to the next. Read levels for groups of barrios, not for one barrio alone.
  • The updated version leaves out one input that has no current source (solid waste facilities other than landfills), and several inputs come from newer sources that define them differently: see How the index is built.
  • Hazards (floods, landslides, sea level rise) are not part of the index, as the white paper decided; the observatory's map shows them as separate layers, and a barrio's pop-up gives its landslide susceptibility beside its levels.

ruleHow the index is built

Hunter College built the Risk and Vulnerability Indicators (RVI) for Foundation for Puerto Rico's WCRP program and described them in a white paper (December 2021); the dashboard data were published in March 2022. The observatory updates them with the same method on the latest five-year Census survey and on current EPA, housing, street and hospital data.

  1. Percentiles. Each input becomes a percentile among the barrios that have a value: the share of those barrios with a lower value, from 0 to 1. A higher percentile always means more vulnerable (for example, more poverty or fewer homes with internet; for street intersections, fewer intersections).
  2. Areas. Within an area, the percentiles of its inputs are added and the sum is ranked again among the barrios. Infrastructure first groups its inputs in two parts (telecommunications and transportation), each ranked, then adds the two. The white paper describes housing in two parts too, but the published housing index is the plain sum of its three inputs (see the check below), so the updated version adds them. A barrio that lacks one input gets the sum of the inputs it has, scaled up to the full number: the white paper's rule for education, where 21 barrios with nobody aged 3 to 24 have their no-diploma percentile doubled.
  3. Overall. The six area percentiles are added with equal weights and the sum is ranked again.
  4. Levels. The index is cut in fifths: 1 (0 to 0.20, low), 2 (medium-low), 3 (medium), 4 (medium-high), 5 (above 0.80, high). Barrios with no residents have no value (14 in the original).
