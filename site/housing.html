FPR Research in Data · Study 01

The State of Housing in Puerto Rico

An analysis of the factors that impact housing and rent prices in Puerto Rico and proposed strategies to increase housing affordability. Foundation for Puerto Rico, 2023, Advancing Equitable Neighborhood Development grant, funded in part by NALCAB, the National Association for Latino Community Asset Builders. Every table in the study is shown below as published, then extended to all 78 municipalities with the Census Bureau's data for every year from 2010 to 2025. home_work2 · Housing stock, vacancy and weekend homes

1.6 million units, over 370,000 vacant, an estimated 300,000 dilapidated or abandoned, and fewer households than homes (Section 2). Vacancy by municipality and year, the share of units the Census classes as "for seasonal, recreational or occasional use" (the closest public proxy for weekend homes and short-term rentals), and the "other vacant" units that are neither for sale or rent nor seasonal: the usual public stand-in for abandoned housing.

payments3 · The housing cost burden

HUD calls a household cost-burdened when it pays more than 30% of income for housing. The study found 52% of renters and 38% of mortgaged owners burdened (Tables 3 and 4). Below, the island-wide distribution for the selected year, rebuilt from the ACS, then each municipality.

real_estate_agent4 · Home ownership affordability

Table 6 of the study converts median income into the largest 30-year mortgage a household can carry at 30% of income. Against a median home value of $111,800 and asking prices of $90,000 and up, most of the population could not buy. The published table, then the same arithmetic for the selected year and for every municipality: the affordability ratio is the median home value divided by that maximum loan (above 1.0 means the typical home is out of reach for the typical household). The loan uses the study's fixed 5% rate in every year, so years differ only through prices and incomes.

Median income vs median home value by municipality ACS

Each dot is a municipality. The line is where the median home exactly equals the maximum affordable loan; dots above it are unaffordable for the median household.

landscape10 · Land use: where housing can go

The study's first strategy is zoning that allows mixed use and density in areas already served by infrastructure (Section 3.1). The Plan de Uso de Terrenos 2015 classifies every hectare as urban, urbanizable, rústico común or especially protected. The share of each municipality already urban, the land set aside for growth, and how much of its tourism supply sits on protected rural land. The plan is a single 2015 snapshot, so these maps do not change with the year. The polygons themselves are a layer in the Data Observatory.

How to cite this page. Foundation for Puerto Rico, FPR Research in Data: The State of Housing in Puerto Rico, PR Data Observatory, , https://observatory.foundationpr.org/housing.html. Data: US Census Bureau (decennial census, Population Estimates, American Community Survey), FEMA OpenFEMA; Junta de Planificación PUT 2015; FPR (2023) study tables.
Population. The 2010 and 2020 censuses are counts for April 1. Every other year is the Census Bureau's estimate for July 1: 2011–2019 from Vintage 2020 (built on the 2010 census and never revised after the 2020 count, so the series steps up in 2020) and 2021–2025 from Vintage 2025 (built on the 2020 census). Age structure comes from the same estimate files.
American Community Survey. Five-year estimates for every window from 2006–2010 to 2020–2024, read directly from the Census Bureau summary files (no API key) and labelled by the last year of the window. Neighbouring windows share four of five years of sample, so for real change compare windows that do not overlap (2012, 2017, 2022). Medians and counts carry the Census Bureau's margin of error at 90% confidence; shares and rates carry margins computed with the Bureau's formulas. The 2021–2025 window arrives in December 2026. Tables used: B01002, B01003, B15002, B17001, B19013, B19049, B19301, B23025, B25001, B25002, B25003, B25004, B25007, B25010, B25014, B25024, B25035, B25064, B25070, B25071, B25077, B25088, B25091, B25119. Incomes and values are in each survey period's dollars, not adjusted for inflation, as in the study.
Census-day counts. Housing units, occupied and vacant for 2010 and 2020 come from the PL 94-171 redistricting files (table H1). Residents per unit divides each census's population by its housing units.
Affordability method (Table 6). 30% of median income for housing, 78% of that to principal and interest (the study's own proportion, $430 of $549), 30-year loan at 5%, which reproduces the study's $80,181 maximum for Puerto Rico's $21,967 median to within about $60. The rate is held at 5% in every year so that years differ only through prices and incomes; actual mortgage rates ranged from under 4% to over 7% over this period. Ratio = median home value ÷ maximum loan.
FEMA. Households approved for Individual Assistance and dollars approved (owners plus renters), summed by municipality and the calendar year of the disaster declaration. Counts are of approvals, not of damaged homes.
Published figures. Tables 2, 6, 7 and 10 and the headline numbers are reproduced as printed in the study (© Foundation for Puerto Rico 2023); live versions of Tables 2, 3, 4, 6 and 7 sit beside them. The study was produced under NALCAB’s Advancing Equitable Neighborhood Development planning grant.
